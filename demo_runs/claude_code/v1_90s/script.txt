Most people will tell you this is a wedding portrait of a pregnant bride.
Look closer, because it is probably neither.
Her green gown was a stock fertility pose, not a sign of pregnancy.
The fur lining is a boast, a billboard for Bruges luxury.
The dog at her feet echoes medieval tombs, where dogs lay at a dead woman's feet to guide her into the afterlife.
And Costanza Trenta, the likely sitter, had died in 1433, a year before the painting was finished.
The convex mirror holds two visitors in the doorway, perhaps the painter himself.
Ten tiny roundels of Christ's Passion circle the glass.
Every scene after Christ's death sits on Costanza's side.
Compare it with Van Eyck's Canon van der Paele altarpiece, painted the same year.
The same mastery of rugs and rich cloth runs through both.
But the clue that matters most is in the hands.
Giovanni holds hers so lightly, as if she were already slipping away.
Pull back, and the whole picture changes.
It reads less like a wedding record than a memorial to a love that outlasts death.
