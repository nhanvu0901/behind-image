## COLD_OPEN
Most people will tell you that this is a wedding portrait, and that the bride is pregnant. It is a lovely story, and guidebooks have repeated it for generations. But look at what is actually in front of us, because almost none of it holds up.
Jan van Eyck finished the Arnolfini Portrait in 1434, and it has puzzled art historians ever since. It is small, an oil painting on an oak panel just eighty-two centimetres tall and sixty wide, yet it crams in more evidence than most rooms full of paintings.
Notice the mirror on the back wall, and the Latin words painted just above it, which say that Jan van Eyck was here, in 1434. A painter does not usually sign his name like a witness. So who was he watching, and what exactly was he witnessing?
Over the next few minutes we will look at this picture in three layers: what we can see with our own eyes, what the documents and historians actually tell us, and how all of that might be read, bearing in mind that no painting has ever held only one meaning.
## THE MERCHANT CITY
To understand the couple, we first have to understand their city. In the early fifteenth century, booming trade produced a new rung on the social ladder, a class of rich and powerful merchants, and nowhere were they more at home than Bruges, in Flanders, which is today part of northern Belgium.
Bruges was an international crossroads, where wool, spices, silk and furs arrived from every direction, and where ambitious people came from across the known world to make their fortunes. One of those families came from Lucca, in Tuscany, and was called Arnolfini.
As the merchants got richer, their hunger for prestige grew with them, and the surest sign that you had arrived was to be painted, which in the 1430s meant being painted by one particular man.
Jan van Eyck was a trailblazer of Northern Renaissance painting. In 1425 he took a post as painter to Philip the Good, the Duke of Burgundy, and by 1431 he had settled in Bruges, where in 1432 he finished the enormous Ghent Altarpiece, yet he kept accepting private clients, and portraits remained central to his career.
So a portrait by Van Eyck was far more than a likeness. In that decade, no purchase announced your success more loudly.
## THE ARNOLFINIS
Who bought this one? A surviving inventory entry points to a merchant named Giovanni di Nicolao Arnolfini, who had taken Costanza Trenta as his wife in 1426. His fortune came from silks and other luxury cloth, along with tapestries and precious objects.
The marriage produced no recorded children, and Costanza was dead by 1433, a full year before Van Eyck finished this panel.
We should be honest about the limits here. Other scholars name a different Arnolfini, or doubt that the woman can be identified at all, and Van Eyck painted a separate portrait of a man from this family, now in Berlin, a few years later. The names are probable, not proven.
What is certain is how unusual the picture is. It shows two people who were neither royalty nor nobility, in their own home, at a time when almost every such picture was made for a court or a church.
For many years the picture was read as a marriage portrait, a kind of visual contract with the figures in the mirror acting as witnesses. But without any evidence that Giovanni remarried, a memorial to Costanza starts to look like the stronger reading, and I find that the more convincing of the two.
Look down at the dog at her feet. In art, dogs usually stand for loyalty, and that may be all this one means. Yet on late medieval tombs, sculptors often placed a dog by a woman's feet, because people believed it would go with her into the afterlife.
Portraits of the dead were not unusual either. Titian, Holbein and Bronzino each painted some, relying on death masks, drawings, descriptions or older likenesses to capture a face that was no longer there.
Whatever else it does, this double portrait pins a prosperous couple firmly to their place in society.
## FASHIONS AND FABRICS
Clothes shape identity, then as now, and here every thread is a statement.
What strikes you first about Costanza is that green dress, bunched up in her hand at the front. People took that for pregnancy, but artists of the time used exactly this pose to signal fertility, which was prized in a wife.
The colour alone is an announcement. To get wool that deep and even, a team of dyers had to labour for weeks, combining plants, herbs and imported spices in a long series of chemical steps.
Now look at the lining. Inside, the gown is faced with the pale belly fur of squirrels, called miniver, and where an ordinary woman's gown took around six hundred pelts, one of this size took as many as two thousand.
Notice how carefully she handles blue. Because blue was so costly, it belonged to the Virgin Mary, so Costanza shows just a flash of blue underdress beneath the fur, which signals money without boasting about it.
Giovanni is dressed with the same care. His clothes are plum and black, dyed by an even pricier process than the green, his hat is straw, woven in tiny plaits, the newest fashion out of Italy, and his velvet tabard, made from silk, nods to the weavers of his home city, and is lined with the pelts of roughly a hundred pine martens from Russia.
His boots are such supple leather that they show the outline of each foot. And in the lower left corner, you can spot the pointed overshoes called pattens, caked with mud from a recent walk.
An abandoned pair of shoes was often read as a symbol of a sacred moment. But they also remind us that this is a real room, on a real day.
## THE ROOM
The room will strike us as strange, and it is. Everything in it was picked to signal comfort and rank, without seeming too grand for a merchant.
Look at the bed behind the couple. A bed suggests a bedroom, yet bedrooms were private, almost holy places, and the mirror shows two visitors being welcomed in. So this is a reception room, where a costly bed stood on show and was never slept in, purely to impress.
Further back is a bench called a settle, carved with grotesque creatures, with a red velvet cushion on top. Comfort was rare in those days, so a cushion counted as a luxury, and the carved monsters, thought to frighten off evil spirits, sit right above the couple's joined hands.
Beside it stands a tall-backed chair, topped by a woman riding a dragon. That could be Saint Martha, patron saint of housewives, and the brush hanging nearby is her emblem.
Now look at the floor. Van Eyck loved painting rugs, because they let him show off his command of texture and pattern, and this oriental carpet is beautifully done. Such rugs often appear in pictures of the Virgin Mary, so it may hint at Costanza's piety.
Set it beside the carpet in the altarpiece he painted for Canon van der Paele, finished the very same year. The same patient looking runs through both, down to the way light settles in the pile.
Beside the mirror hangs a string of amber prayer beads, the sort of present a groom gave his bride, and each bead holds its own highlight, with a green silk thread, probably laid in with a one-hair brush, running through them.
Finally, let your eye drift to the window. Glass was expensive, and the oranges on the sill were imports that only the very rich could buy. Study the single orange, and you will find that it throws a shadow and is mirrored by the windowpane, a perfect still life painted before anyone thought of still life as a subject.
## THE MIRROR
And now to the most intriguing object in the picture. The mirror pulls the whole room together, adding depth and life to an otherwise quiet scene.
In the fifteenth century glass mirrors were high technology, and they were usually convex, like this one. But a mirror so wide could not actually be made then, so Van Eyck simply enlarged it.
Look closely at the reflection. There is the couple, seen from the back, and two more people stepping through a doorway that must lie on our side of the picture. One of them, dressed in blue, is sometimes said to be Van Eyck, though nobody can prove it, and the other could stand for us.
Mirrors were also used in art to suggest purity, since a clean surface shows the truth, so this one may be there to underline the sanctity of the union.
Now notice the frame. Ten tiny roundels, picturing the suffering and rising of Christ, are the picture's only explicit religious references, and even at that size the detail is remarkable.
Their positions matter. Every episode that comes after the death of Christ is placed beside Costanza: he is lifted from the cross, laid in the tomb, rises again, and descends to free the souls waiting in limbo.
And one more detail, from the chandelier above. Up in the chandelier, one candle is lit and another has been snuffed out, the dead one hanging above Costanza. Some read the flame as the eye of God, or as a wedding custom, but if you accept the memorial theory, it contrasts the living with the dead.
## THE ALCHEMIST
Saying that a painting has one single meaning would be reckless, but a handful of clues do lean towards the memorial interpretation, starting with the dog by her skirt, then the small scenes on her half of the frame, and the chance that he is wearing mourning clothes, though dark, costly cloth was also simply fashionable.
Yet the strongest hint is the way Giovanni takes her hand. Look at the joined hands, and notice how light the grip is, as if she might at any moment slip out of his grasp.
Then again, the same gesture has been read as a wedding vow. The painting can carry both readings at once, and perhaps that is why it still keeps us looking.
The real marvel is how Van Eyck handles space, depth, colour and texture, so that we feel we could step through the flat wooden panel into a mirrored world. His contemporaries called him an alchemist, and although he did not invent oil painting, as early writers believed, he certainly improved it.
He knew that some pigments turn almost transparent in oil, so he built the picture from many thin layers, which gives it a glow, and a surface so smooth that it looks untouched. Technical studies show that in places the paint went on quite freely, and there is even a thumbprint close to the dog's shadow.
His reach extended far past the Low Countries, with patrons in England, France and Portugal. He inspired artists from Albrecht Durer, whose self-portrait of 1500 shares that direct gaze, to Velazquez, whose Las Meninas has its own mirror at the back, and on to the Pre-Raphaelites.
## CLOSING
So let us return to the question we started with. Is this a wedding portrait of a pregnant bride? Almost certainly not.
It may record a marriage, it may be a memorial, and perhaps it is a little of both, but if we follow the clues the painter left us, the dog, the roundels, the candle, the dark clothes and the grip of a hand, it begins to look like something deeper, a portrait of a love that outlasts death.
And the man who was there to witness it signed his name above the mirror, so that we would know.
